Week 6 · From counts to meaning

Five ways to say “close”

Week 6 gave us five representations of language and, therefore, five definitions of close: raw counts, TF-IDF, topic mixtures, PPMI context vectors and Word2Vec. This week's go nuts post asks one question — how much does the representation change the answer? — and lets you watch it change, one Marvel character at a time, with the actual sentences underneath to keep you honest.

The question

↑

The course page splits the day cleanly: documents are represented by their words (TF-IDF, topic mixtures), and later words are represented by their contexts (PPMI, Word2Vec). Each representation ranks neighbors, but no two of them rank alike — and the week's recurring warning is that a cosine without a close reading proves nothing. So instead of five figures, we want one interface that asks the same question of every representation and shows its paperwork:

Pick a character. Who is closest to them — under counts, under TF-IDF, under topic mixtures, under PPMI, under Word2Vec — and is each match driven by a shared name, shared boilerplate, or a shared story?

Structural truth is never farther than a badge: the week-1 Wikipedia links and the week-4 Louvain communities ride along, so the classic mismatch — textually close, structurally far — is visible at a glance.

The word half

The character's TF-IDF fingerprint (proper names removed) becomes a set of entry points into the word-level world. Each chip shows the same word as a row of the word–context matrix under raw counts, PPMI, and a Marvel-trained Word2Vec — read the three columns top-to-bottom and watch hammer travel from power, to, with to thor, asgard.

Data: 303 Marvel Wikipedia pages (727k tokens). Document neighbors: cosine under each representation, top 10. Word2Vec: gensim skip-gram, 100 dims, window 5, min_count 5, seed 0. PPMI: contexts within ±5 sentence words, log₂ of observed vs. expected. Topics: LDA, 8 topics, seed 0 (CountVectorizer, min_df 5, max_df 0.5). Community overlay: week-4 Louvain (seed 42). Everything was computed once offline and frozen into three JSON payloads; the page is a pure reader.

What we saw, with the text as referee

↑

Counts are desperate measures

Ask Wolverine for raw-count neighbors and you get the X-corner: Cyclops, Quicksilver, Hellion — every character page talks the same Wikipedia dialect of was, series, character and team, so raw cosine mostly measures page style. On average only 1.9 of every character's ten raw-count neighbors are actually linked in the week-1 network (≈0.3 for ten random pages).

TF-IDF sharpens the answer — and finds the Frost problem

Switch to TF-IDF and the network agreement jumps to 4.0 of 10 — with no stopword list at all. The ranking also gets sharper sentences behind it: Wolverine's top neighbors share logan, adamantium, mutant — and the pattern that near-duplicate pages top the list (Ultimate Wolverine) is real structure Wikipedia itself created.

But TF-IDF has a famous failure mode, and Storm shows it: her closest page is the Human Torch, whose point of contact is the surname storm — and who she is not linked to, while 8 of her 9 other top-10 neighbors are real links. A rare word is, by definition, informative — and a surname is a rare word. It's the course's own caution, reproduced in our numbers: the vector knows frequency, not meaning.

Topic mixtures: structure without sentences

LDA agrees with the network far less than TF-IDF does (1.3 of 10), and that's not a bug: a mixture similarity measures what kind of page the neighbor is, not which exact words it uses. Toggle remove proper names and the topics turn from name lists into themes (symbiotes & hosts, gods & vampires, and the honest media boilerplate). Whether that's more “meaning” than a name list is exactly the judgment call we want readers to make with the sentence drawer open.

The word half: same lemma, three stories

↑

The word panel riffs on the week's dog/puppy/democracy demo with corpus-sized words. For hammer (87 occurrences):

counts neighbors power, to, with
PPMI neighbors thor, falls, bill
Word2Vec neighbors odinson, truck, weaken

Raw counts put the furniture of every sentence next to it. PPMI removes the furniture and leaves the myth — Mjölnir's household (thor, bill, asgard). Word2Vec lands odinson at the top and then, with the confidence a 727k-token corpus deserves, adds truck and weaken — every neighbor list this week ends the same way: back to the text.

And PPMI has its own vice: the's top context dimensions are junk rare words (kommander, diff…). Discounting common pairs rewards any unexpected co-occurrence, including editorial crumbs. These sparse vectors are the same argument from the document half, now at word level: no representation is telling you the truth about meaning; every one is plausible machinery that you have to audit.

The closest strangers

The list below the explorable ranks the pairs with the highest TF-IDF cosine of any unlinked pair — our corpus's best candidates for textually close, structurally far:

#1 is the jackpot: Emma Frost ↔ Jack Frost, cos = 0.44, driven almost entirely by frost and frost's — the week's favorite example, reproduced in our numbers. #2–#5 are the Strikeforce: Morituri cluster: teammates who share a comic, its creators and rare vocabulary, and simply never got links between their pages. That family is real structure, detectable only from text — the split between surname matches and teammate matches is one a cosine can suggest but only a reader can make.

What this explorable can't do

↑

One question of one corpus, with one frozen run of every model. The LDA solution depends on seed and topic count; Word2Vec on our 727k tokens is small enough that neighbor lists reshuffle between library versions; the one-fit-per-representation framing is precisely the lesson we keep drumming — these are instances, not the method. And a frozen page can take you to the sentences, but it cannot take the final step for you: deciding, with your own eyes, which matches are names, which are boilerplate, and which are real.
