How it works

↑

Each villain's Wikipedia page is turned into a TF-IDF vector (5,000 features, unigrams and bigrams, stop words and common name fragments removed). NMF (Non-negative Matrix Factorization) then decomposes the document-term matrix into 10 topics — each topic is a weighted list of words, and each document is a weighted mixture of topics. A villain's dominant topic is the one with the highest weight.

This is a different lens from the divergence explorer: topics group villains by shared vocabulary (what their pages literally say), while the MiniLM embeddings group them by semantic similarity (what their pages mean). The two often agree — the Spider-Man topic and the Spider-Man semantic neighborhood overlap — but not always.

The cross-lens: divergent pairs within a topic

↑

The most interesting pairs are where all three lenses agree on meaning but disagree on structure: two villains in the same topic (similar vocabulary), semantically close (high MiniLM cosine), but structurally far (long or no path in the hyperlink network). These are characters whose pages talk about the same things, read as similar to a language model — yet Wikipedia's editors never connected them.

Click a topic card, then look at the divergent pairs within topic list. For example, the X-Men/mutant topic contains Blood Spider ↔ Spider-Woman (cos 0.69, path 3) — both mutant-era Spider-characters whose pages share vocabulary about clones, costumes, and the Spider-verse, yet neither page links to the other.

Why does this happen? Topic models group by word overlap; the hyperlink network groups by editorial choice. A page about a 1990s Spider-clone and the Spider-Woman disambiguation page share words like spider, costume, clone, Marvel — but the Wikipedia editor who wrote one didn't think to link to the other. The gap between topic and structure is a map of missed connections.
