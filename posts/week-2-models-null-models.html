Week 2 · Models & null models

Is Spider-Man an accident?

Testing the Marvel network against three different kinds of "nothing special going on here," then growing a network that tries to build a Marvel of its own.

Intro Random nulls Shuffle test Grow a network Friendship paradox Takeaways

Setting up the question

Week 1 found a heavily skewed network: 303 characters, mean in-degree 5.89, and one character — Spider-Man — cited by 106 others, almost double the runner-up (Hulk, 64). That kind of shape invites a specific question: is a hub that large just what happens when you scatter 1,784 links across 303 nodes, or does it take something more deliberate — an actual rich-get-richer mechanism — to produce it at all?

This week adds no new data. Everything below runs on the same 303 nodes and 1,784 directed edges from data/week1, computed live in your browser. Four questions, in order: could pure chance produce this? Once every character's exact number of links is frozen and only who connects to whom is randomized, what changes and what doesn't? Can a growth model that explicitly favors the well-connected reproduce it, and what does it get wrong? And what does this shape mean for an individual hero looking at their own connections?

Could chance alone produce this?

The simplest null model treats every possible directed pair of characters as equally, independently likely to link: an Erdős–Rényi random graph with the same 303 nodes and 1,784 edges as the real network. If a hub like Spider-Man's 106 in-links is just what falls out of scattering that many edges at random, an Erdős–Rényi (ER) draw should occasionally come close.

It doesn't. Offline, we ran 300 independent ER draws: across all of them, the biggest hub's in-degree ranged from 11 to 18 (mean 13.9, standard deviation 1.2). Spider-Man's in-degree is 106 — about 6× larger than the largest of 300 random draws, and roughly 74 standard deviations above the random mean. What survives once degree is held fixed?

ER is a strong null — it throws away how many links each character has, too. A tighter question: if every character keeps exactly their real in-degree and out-degree, and only the wiring — who links to whom — is scrambled, does the network still look distinctive?

We test this with a degree-preserving directed shuffle: repeatedly take a chain of edges a→b, b→c, c→d and rewire it to a→c, c→b, b→d, which changes who's connected to whom but leaves every node's in-degree and out-degree exactly as they were. Two statistics, defined precisely:

  • Clustering coefficient — the average local clustering coefficient on the undirected projection of the network (an edge exists between two characters if either one's article links to the other's), averaged over all 303 nodes, with degree-<2 nodes contributing 0. This is the same undirected projection used below in the friendship paradox section. Real value: 0.3075.
  • Reciprocity — mutual directed edges ÷ total directed edges = 700 ÷ 1,784 = 0.3924.

Offline, we ran 300 shuffle realizations (3,000 swaps each) for both statistics at once. Clustering's null mean was 0.180 (sd 0.009) — the real value sits about 14 standard deviations above it, and not one of the 300 shuffles reached it (empirical p ≈ 0). Reciprocity's null mean was 0.069 (sd 0.007) — the real value sits about 44 standard deviations above that, again beyond all 300 shuffles. Both survive the shuffle: whatever produces this network's triangle-heavy structure and its mutual-citation rate, it is not explained by degree alone.

Run the same test yourself: pick a statistic, run some shuffles, and watch the null build up.

Two things worth being precise about. First, the distinction: the ER test above asks "what if edges were placed with no constraint at all?" — this shuffle asks the narrower question, "what if the exact degree sequence stayed fixed, and only the wiring were randomized?" Second, a caveat on the method: this swap preserves each node's directed in-degree and out-degree exactly — that's what it's built to do. It does not guarantee each node's undirected-projection degree stays fixed, because a swap can turn a reciprocal pair into a one-way link (or the reverse), which changes how many distinct undirected neighbors a node has. That's the right null for reciprocity and directed structure; it's a slightly looser null for the undirected clustering number than a literal undirected-degree-preserving shuffle would be, and we're flagging that rather than hiding it.

One more thing we checked offline, but didn't turn into a control above: whether the 9-character island Week 1 found ever reconnects to the giant component under this shuffle. Across all 300 offline realizations, the giant component's size never changed — it was exactly 277, every single time (standard deviation 0). That isn't a close call decided by chance; it's guaranteed by how the swap works. A swap only ever rewires edges along an existing directed walk (a→b→c→d), and that walk can never cross between two weakly connected components, because there are no edges between them to walk across. So this particular null model can't even ask "would the island reconnect by chance" — the component partition is invariant under it by construction, not by result. That's why it isn't offered as a third toggle above: a histogram with zero variance wouldn't be testing anything.

Growing a network that favors the popular

Preferential attachment: start small and grow to 303 nodes one at a time; each newcomer sends m outgoing links to existing characters, chosen with probability proportional to how many in-links that character already has. This is the classic rich-get-richer growth mechanism — specifically its directed, citation-network form (sometimes called the Price model), which fits here since the real network is directed with clearly different in- and out-degree roles, unlike the usual undirected Barabási–Albert setup. Out-degree is exactly m for every non-seed node by construction — the growth ingredient; in-degree accumulates by preference — the rich-get-richer ingredient.

This is a generative hypothesis being tested against the data, not a claim about how the Wikipedia article network actually got written.

At m=6 (chosen to land close to the real edge count: 6×303 ≈ 1,818 vs. the real 1,784), we ran 50 trials offline. Every single one produced a bigger hub than Spider-Man: max in-degree ranged from 116 to 166 (mean 141), against the real network's 106. Preferential attachment doesn't just reproduce a big hub here — at this m, it reliably overshoots it. Lowering m brings the model's hub size down (m=3: range 70–137, mean 99, much closer to 106; m=2: range 44–125, mean 79) but moves the total edge count further from the real 1,784 — no single m we tried matches both the network's density and its hub size at once. And in every trial, at every m: one connected component, zero isolated characters — versus the real network's 17 isolates and a separate 9-character island. A model where everyone who ever joins immediately attaches to the network can't produce someone nobody ever mentions.

Are your friends more popular than you?

Exact definitions: take the undirected projection (a link either way counts as one connection — the same projection used for clustering above). A character's degree is their number of distinct neighbors; their average neighbor degree is the mean degree of those neighbors. Averaged over the whole network, ⟨k⟩ = 9.465, while the edge-weighted mean neighbor degree ⟨k²⟩⁄⟨k⟩ = 22.08 — more than double. That gap is the friendship paradox, and it's worth stating plainly: ⟨k²⟩⁄⟨k⟩ is a pure function of the degree sequence itself, so it's mathematically guaranteed to survive a degree-preserving shuffle — it isn't something this page needs to test, and we haven't added a shuffle control here for that reason.

What isn't guaranteed is what it looks like character by character. Of the 286 characters with at least one connection, 250 (87.4%) have neighbors who are on average more connected than they are; 36 (12.6%) are themselves more connected than their average neighbor. Only two characters are never out-popularitied by any of their own neighbors — their degree is greater than or equal to every one of their neighbors': Spider-Man, and, inside the isolated Morituri island, Radian.

What the models get right, and what they miss

Pulling the four sections together:

  • Independent random edges (ER) are rejected outright. No realization comes remotely close to the real hub structure — 300 draws topped out at max in-degree 18, against the real 106.
  • Degree alone doesn't explain the clustering or the reciprocity either. Both statistics sit far outside a degree-preserving shuffle's null distribution (z ≈ 14 and z ≈ 44) — there is real relational structure here (character families, teams, mutual citations) beyond what each character's link count would predict. The one property that turned out to be invariant under this shuffle — the giant-component/island split — is invariant by construction, a property of the method rather than a discovery about the Marvel network.
  • Preferential attachment gets the mechanism right and the calibration wrong. A growth-plus-preference model does produce extreme hubs — at any tested m, every trial's hub beat Spider-Man's real in-degree. But no single m matched both the real edge count and the real hub size simultaneously, and the model structurally cannot produce isolated characters or a disconnected island: everyone who joins, connects.
  • The friendship paradox holds, strongly, and for two different reasons. At the network level, ⟨k²⟩⁄⟨k⟩ (22.08) more than doubles ⟨k⟩ (9.465) — guaranteed by the degree sequence alone. At the individual level, 87.4% of connected characters personally experience it — but exactly which characters serve as "the popular friend" for whom depends on the network's actual wiring, not just on how many links everyone has.

So: no, Spider-Man isn't an accident of scale or density. Something resembling rich-get-richer growth is a much better fit for the real hub structure than any of the random baselines — but it's a generative hypothesis this data is consistent with, not something these four tests prove actually happened, and even the best-fitting version of it still can't explain the 17 characters nobody bothered to link to at all.
