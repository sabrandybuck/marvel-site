A corpus instead of a graph

↑

The week 1 network told us who mentions whom; the week 5 release is the actual prose — the full plain text of the same 303 Wikipedia articles (4.5 MB frozen on 2026-08-26). Tokenizing everything — lowercase, keep apostrophes, strip punctuation — gives 727,440 tokens containing 26,952 distinct words. The median page is only 1,238 tokens, but the corpus is front-heavy: Spider-Man's article alone is 10,293 tokens, 14× the median.

Heaps' law of the Marvel universe

↑

Heaps' law says a growing corpus keeps meeting new words at a slow, predictable decay: V ≈ Nβ. Fitting our linked-first curve in log–log space gives β ≈ 0.51 — right in the classic 0.4–0.6 range expected for natural text. But the exponent is only half the story; the order is the other half. The exploration: add the pages one at a time, most-linked character first (in-degree from the week 1 network), and watch the vocabulary grow step by step.

Use the explorable below: pick an ordering, press play or scrub, and hover the curve to ask "what did this page contribute?". The dashed blue band is where 24 random shuffles land (10th–90th percentile); the grey ghost line is another ordering's full curve for comparison.

Where the words actually come from. The linked-first ordering front-loads everything: the very first page (Spider-Man, in-degree 106) deposits 10,293 brand-new words — by page 50, 18,264 of the final 26,952 words (68%) already exist, even though those 50 pages are only 45% of the corpus's tokens. From there the curve flattens into pure repetition: page 20 (Iron Fist) adds 749 new words out of 9,022 tokens (91.7% already seen), page 100 adds 23 out of 806 (97.2% repeated), and the very last page — Zombie, in-degree 0 — still manages 31 words nobody had used.

Playing the order in reverse answers the flip side: vocabulary starts catch-up-slow (minor stub pages talk in plot-specific proper nouns) and picks up speed when the network's hubs finally arrive with their army of words. Alphabetical sits between — it's roughly a random semi-sorted order. The random band is tight: no shuffle order changes where the curve ends, only how much the last hundred pages still have left to teach.

Our answer: Heaps' law holds (β ≈ 0.51), but the interesting part is that the curve's shape is an artifact of who you read first. Famous characters are vocabulary traps — huge, and overwhelmingly reused later; the marginal new word lives on the minor pages, in volume, all the way to the end of the corpus.

Do minor characters bring new words? Yes — at a slightly better rate

↑

Split the corpus into famous pages (in-degree ≥ 7 — the top quartile of the week 1 network: 77 pages) and minor ones (the other 226). Then ask two different questions. First, in the linked-first build: while the whole corpus is being added in that order, how many brand-new words does each bucket deposit, per 1,000 tokens it reads out? Second, an order-independent one: how many words are private to each bucket — found in no page of the other bucket?

Same quantity, two framings, and they disagree about who "carries" the vocabulary. The famous bucket is only 77 pages but carries 418k tokens (58% of the corpus), and because they are added first, they bank a raw 37,563 new words (89.8 per 1,000 tokens) vs the minor bucket's 31,880 (103.1 per 1,000). But the private-vocabulary count — which depends on no ordering — says famous pages are only worth 8,131 words nobody else ever uses, vs 6,558 for the minors; relative to bucket size the minor pages punch at the same weight (21.2 private words per 1,000 tokens vs 19.4).

So: minor characters do mostly repeat famous vocabulary (97–98% of their tokens are already-seen words once the hubs are in), but per token they still introduce just as much genuinely fresh vocabulary — mostly plot nouns, place names and one-off terms the famous pages never need.

Who has the weirdest Wikipedia page?

↑

"Weird" is undefinable in one number — so we defined it in six, using only this week's toolbox, each z-scored across the 303 pages and then averaged into a composite:

  • Lexical diversity (type–token ratio) — a page full of never-repeated words is leaner, louder and stranger than one leaning on "the".
  • Hapax share — of the page's distinct words, the fraction used exactly once: the purest single-number proxy for "one-offs, never explained, never heard from again".
  • Corpus one-off share — the corpus-aware sibling: of the page's tokens, the fraction sitting in words used exactly once anywhere in the 303-page corpus. Immune to page length; a page that owns its vocabulary outright scores high.
  • Low entropy — repetitive pages are monotone and, this time round, weird; counted as −z(entropy).
  • Mean TF-IDF — average TF-IDF weight of all tokens; rewards pages written from words no other hero page uses.
  • Zipf deviation — each page's own frequency–rank curve fitted; the distance of its slope from the ideal −1. Its companion R² is stored too, because fitting a power law to a 200-token page is a noisy business.

The leaderboard below sorts all pages by any single component or the composite, in either direction, and any of them can be inspected: click a row (or search a character) to see all numbers and its most TF-IDF-distinctive words.

Inspecting the winners — real weirdness, or just stubs and armour plating?

↑

The composite crowns Illuminator (+1.72): a 260-token vigilante page with TTR high enough to rival the best stubs and 11.5% of its tokens in words used exactly once anywhere in the corpus. Runners-up follow: Coldblood (a cyborg army of proper nouns), Phil Grayfield (NFL bromance → hero; his page is full of nfl and football), Emrys Killebrew and Happy Sam Sawyer — and then two instructive cases: Baymax (+1.28), the corpus-one-off star riding a 1,553-token page (9.5% isolated tokens, z +4.0 — being "weird" is not just for stubs), and Helix (+1.28), the previous composite champion, whose within-page hapax share still crushes (80%) but whose corpus-isolated share is exactly average (z −0.04): its plot nouns keep reappearing in other Clone Saga pages. Genuinely weird and hanging on: Super Rabbit (+1.21), a 1943 Timely Comics talking animal whose most distinctive words include rabbit and waffles. At the bottom, predictably and comfortably: Spider-Man, Venom, Black Widow — long, boilerplate-thick pages whose vocabulary is half the corpus already.

Was the metric real or artefactual? Both, honestly, and the artifact is the finding: the leaderboard still prefers short pages (TTR and within-page hapax both inflate on stubs), so most of the top-25 are 200–700-token minor characters. Two anti-noise checks separate signal from artifact: the One-offs (corpus) tab ignores page size entirely and crowns Blackwulf (12.0% of tokens in words nobody else's pages ever use, z +5.5, on a fully mid-size 1,022-token page), Illuminator (z +5.2) and — remarkable for its 4,381-token length — Miracleman (z +3.5); and the Zipf tab's R² column filters the stub-noise (Helix's fit R² 0.81 is the worst of the winners; Black Jack Tarr and Super Rabbit keep flat, proper-noun-heavy rank curves at usable sizes, R² 0.92). None of the winners is weird because of formatting or boilerplate — they're weird because a page devoted to one storyline has vocabulary to share with nobody. The metric found real vocabulary isolation; it just also rewards being short while doing so.

Our answer: even six z-scored "weirdness" statistics converge on the same population — plot-noun pages no one links to — but the corpus one-off share redistributes the credit toward pages that own words outright: the stub-gold of Blackwulf, Illuminator, Baymax. Filter by the R² of the Zipf fit and the list becomes defensible; skip the filter and you mostly learn where the corpus's happy little accidental poetry lives (yes, waffles).

The duel: build a corpus by Zipf's rule, one-offs or by Heaps' rule

↑

The metrics of this week answer different questions, so we made them race — via one dropdown, not a wall of buttons. It drives both the manual + Add top-score button and auto-play at once: pick Zipf deviation (the precomputed page whose rank–frequency slope bends hardest away from the ideal −1), corpus one-offs (the remaining page holding the biggest share of its tokens in words used exactly once in the corpus), Heaps gain (defined live, the way Heaps' score must be: the number of brand-new words a page would deposit right now, which depends on everything already added), or a random pick. + One step adds one page by the dropdown's choice, ▶ Play keeps adding at ~10 a second until the corpus is full (switch the dropdown live mid-run); or type any character's name and add it by hand.

Each addition draws that character's own Zipf curve on the log–log chart, behind the merged vocabulary curve of everything you've built so far, with the idealized Zipf curve (slope −1) as the reference. The layers are checkbox toggles; the left panel summarizes the page you just added, including its live marginal gain ΔV.

Watch for the signature heuristic swap: if you top up with Zipf outliers (spiky minor stubs) repeatedly, the merged curve stays far below the ideal line — you keep buying template-stamped vocabulary that is rare in the aggregate but suspiciously concentrated in each page. The Heaps button, by contrast, buys words that already show up in no other page you own — different vocabulary entirely. Which strategy pulls the accumulated corpus closest to the ideal line is the duel; press the buttons and see.

What we take away

↑

This week the Marvel data got its words back, and the corpus behaved exactly like a corpus: Heaps' β ≈ 0.51, two-thirds of the vocabulary banked by the 50 most-linked pages, minor pages paying their share of fresh vocabulary at the same rate everyone else does. And "weird", once arithmetised with six tools, turned out to mean small, plot-specific, and vocabulary-wise on its own — the leaderboard's winners are the characters the network never bothered connecting, and the corpus one-off share adds the ones that own words outright: Blackwulf, Illuminator, Baymax.

The one number that surprised us most: famous pages hold 8,131 words found nowhere else — but the 226 minor pages still beat them on rate (21.2 vs 19.4 per-1,000 tokens). The hubs are where vocabulary accumulates. The margins are where it gets minted.
